# Geometry is your Friend

This is a project I started in earnest at the beginning of 2026, with some vague thoughts put towards it in late 2025.
I started it mostly as a way to work on learning [Lean](https://lean-lang.org/), a modern
proof-assistant/proof-verifier. The main thing about Lean I was interested in what how flexible it is with respect to
syntax and parsing. I've been interested in ATP/Proof assistants for a long time, but I've always bounced off how
frustrating the experience of using them is.

When I write a proof, it is 'gestural' in the sense that I am not trying necessarily to accurately settle every
hypothesis and conclusion, but rather to guide your intuition through and around the potholes so you can complete the
course on your own. Informal proof is about argument and doubt, if I can reasonably doubt something is true, then it
must be established. You repeat this application of doubt until you have exhausted your abilities.

I like to think that I'm pretty good at finding ways to doubt assertions, but obviously, there is a sort of 'Tool
assisted' doubt we can imagine. A computational accountant ensuring that the details of the proof are encoded and
consumed only according to the underlying rules of whatever logical system we operate within. The most straightforward
tool for this is Type Theory, in particular Dependent Type Theory, which allows a pretty natural encoding of formal
logic into the type system.

The problem comes as you build up from here, you start to rapidly acquire a programming problem very familiar to your
average software engineer -- bloat, debt, ill-factoring of code -- all your usual 'enterprise' problems start to show up
almost immediately. The root of these issues is the encoding, you have to write a program equivalent to your 'gestural'
proof, so gestures must be translated in some way to these systems.

ATPs like Rocq (erstwhile Coq) offer relatively little here, they say "just do the DTT and be done." This is valid, but
unsatifying to me. I want to do _math_, not write code. I want it to feel like math when I do it as much as possible. I
want the code to "read well." I want it to be legible to a non-programmer. Not likely to find that with Rocq.

Isabelle, Idris, Agda -- these all offer small alternatives. Isabelle is the most flexible but it operates in a
different paradigm (HOL is not quite the same approach as traditional DTT as far as I understand, but my understanding
of the differences does not go particularly far). Agda is a popular choice for HoTT as I understand but that is deeper
and finer magic than I have yet to attempt.

Lean offers a pretty basic approach to theorem proving, but a _stunningly_ flexible syntax extension tool. In fact, it
offers like, four or five different ways to modify syntax. I wrote a friend a relatively long
[letter](../../letters/shaeerisson-2-mar-2026/) about it not so long ago; I have since gone even further with syntax
manipulation. It turns out modern LLMs are _extremely_ good at mucking around with the Elab/Metaprogramming subsystems
of Lean and let you do some [pretty amazing](TODO: Link to atlas) stuff.

So I started digging through Greenberg and proving theorems, but I encountered trouble.

# The two hardest problems in computer science

> The two hardest problems in computer science are
>
> 1. Caching
> 2. Naming
> 3. Off-by-one errors.

When proving theorems about geometric diagrams, you are confronted immediately with the fact that they are all
constructed out of various species of lines and points named on those lines. The primary problem of Geometry, at least
from Leans perspective, is finding and validating premises under all the various valid permutations of points in that
diagram.

The problem comes from how lines are _named_. In the inciting model, a `Line` is defined as Greenberg has, as a set of
points. Greenberg makes lots of use of Set theory as an intuitive tool for reasoning about the structure of points named
on the line, but does so at two costs:

1. Lines are sets of points and have no notion of 'the set of all named points incident to this line' -- those are
   separate hypotheses which must be maintained and bookkept separately.
2. We have to account for _all_ of the points on the line, even though we don't care about most of them.

In reality, the points not named by a diagram are unimportant. We only care about points we can _name_, which is to say,
points which are in some sense _constrained_. An unconstrained point offers no real value to any geometric proof. If I
say, "Here is Pappus' theorem" and "Here is Pappus' theorem but with an extra unconstrained point," we have learned
nothing.

The problem arises in this situation. Say you have a abstract line `L`, this is intuitively 'some collection of
collinear points.' Let's say we also have two (distinct) named points `A` and `B`, and two hypothesis `A on L` and `B on
L`. We can then pretty readily see that `L = AB` where `AB` is 'the line through A and B'.

From Lean's perspective, though, we have:

```lean
L : Set Point
A B : Point
AonL : A \in L
BonL : B \in L
```

as a proofstate, and we would have to prove `L = (line A B)` (where `line` is a set constructor) and maintain it as a
hypothesis in the environment. This is fine for two points, but what about 4 or 5 points? The _Line Separation
Property_'s proof in fact uses 5 collinear points and an auxillary and essentially chases these named lines around to
associate them with abstract ones. It's a nightmare of slowly cajoling everything back to some 'normal form' which is ad
hoc, proof local, and hard to see through as the proofstate bloats.

## Proofstate Bloat

It gets worse, because a bloated proofstate is also materially _slower_ to work with. Most tactics involve some
introspection on the current proofstate to proceed. A proofstate is a structure that is something like:


```
Proofstate {
    hypotheses: [Terms]
    goals: [Holes]
}
```

The object is to take a hole from the `goals` side of the state, and construct a term from the available hypotheses and
other theorems to construct a term of a type that matches the hole. Proofstates can be manipulated either by replacing
goals, introducing new intermediate hypotheses (by combining old ones), or by fulfilling goals. A `tactic` is a small
metaprogram that does this. Tactics can be simple 'use this hypothesis here' (e.g., `exact` or `apply`), or they can be
complex, examining the proofstate itself and making heuristic decisions on how to proceed (e.g., `aesop`, `simp`, or
`tauto`).

A tactic might create a new hypothesis from an old one. For instance, in GIYF, I have a simple helper struct/syntax
tool, `distinct`. This takes a list of terms and essentially states 'these are pairwise distinct'. I have a tactics
`separate` and `distinguish` which searches the proofstate for `distinct` hypotheses and replaces them with `X \ne Y`
hypotheses, and then dispatches goals of simple inequality (respectively). If there are more items in the proofstate,
then this search takes longer. This is exacerbated if the hypotheses the tactic adds may need to be recursively added to
the search set so that more hypotheses can be added to eventually reach a fixpoint or close all available goals. The
bigger the proofstate, the more recursion/searching, the slower the proof system gets. This is mostly a practical
problem but one that rapidly saps any joy of working with the tool.

In the situation with lines, we can imagine a line with `n` named points has `O(n^2)` ways to name that line by it's
points, plus some (relevant for us at our scales) constant number of alternative 'abstract' names (where naming by
incident points is, in some sense, 'concrete'). This means we would have `O(n^2 + k)` total names, and since we would
need to establish pairwise _equality_ of these named lines, we have `O(n^4 + k^2)` total hypotheses added (again, `n` is
generally not much bigger than `k` at our scale, and I'm abusing `O` notation here, but hopefully the idea is clear,
this is untenable for any nontrivial choices of `n` or `k`).

# Tangling

A simple insight would be, "What if Lines knew about the points named on them directly?" We would have a structure which
grows from step-to-step of the proofstate. When we show incidence, we 'glue' a point to the `Line` structure and ignore
all the unnamed points. What are the properties of such a line?

Well, obviously for any line, we can name a new, distinct point on it (by Hilbert's I-2 and B-2).

We can also 'forget' a point exists (there is no axiom here, we're just ignoring information we have).

Incidence on a line is equivalent to a proof that the named point is collinear with any two named points on the line.
Note that this disallows a 'bare' proof of a point existing on an abstract line. That is to say, given `P` and `L`, we
cannot necessarily prove `P on L` without creating known points on `L` first. This is a good thing, in my opinion,
because it better models constructive geometry. We could construct a situation where we have `L` and `P on L` with no
other points, but we have to _start_ with `L` and name `P` on it, or _start_ with `P` and draw `L` through it, but we
cannot resolve the situation without the construction.

If we have multiple named points on a line, they must be in some coherent order (the betweeness axioms ensure this).
This is just a lattice acting on the points, with the ordering given by ambient betweeness conditions (a betweeness is a
strict ordering of three points up to mirror symmetry, more on that in a second).

We can also 'glue' two different lines together in an 'intersection' by having the _same_ named point appear on more
than one line. We can readily show that if we glue two lines in two distinct places, we have named the same line.

Suddenly a structure was starting to appear, in a sense, a geometric diagram considering only Betweeness and Incidence
is really just arguments about interactions of certain structures. I call this structure a 'Tangling of points on a
Line', or just a 'Tangling.'

The structure is _very_ simple, it's a finite set of points, and a finite set of betweeness conditions (from which we
can build a set of possible lattices that can be imposed on the points. Proving incidence in a tangling means the
lattice gains another member.

This also opens up an interesting new ability -- if the number of possible lattices is small, we can _dispatch_ over
them, that is, treat it as a disjunction and deal with each case under a full ordering of the underlying named points.
This makes some proofs really natural, and reflects the 'gestural' argument from before. Especially when we can show
there is no valid ordering -- the proof is over, _ex falso quod libet_.

I started, then, to wonder about this structure directly, and that is what led me down the next step of the rabbit hole.

# A day at the beach

Let's think about Geometry from scratch. We might start by considering our tools -- we have a straightedge, we have a
compass, we have a pencil. What can we construct?

Segments. Circles. Points.

That's it. That's everything we can construct as a primitive. Everything else is some kind of weakening of these basic
items. A Ray is a Segment that lost a bound, a Line lost both bounds.

What if we only had the pencil?

We can _still_ talk about points, lines, and circles, we just can't _draw_ them anymore. The points can still be
constrained, only our constructions are limited by the lack of tools (and honestly not by much, a compass alone is
sufficient to produce all of Euclidean Geometry; as is a Straightedge with a fixed circle preconstructed).
Fundamentally, every diagram really comes back to arguments about points that are 'contained' by some other condition,
either colinearity and boundedness (for line species) or cocircularity (for circles). Arcs are the 'odd one out', but
only in visualization, functionally they are no different than a segment, and obey the same symmetries.

This isn't a deep thing to notice, but it is useful -- the only things that really separate these objects are:

1. Boundedness
2. Symmetry

Line species have linear symmetry -- that means they are the same up to a mirroring of all the points. Circles have
cyclic symmetry -- we can't necessarily pick a left or rightmost point, and a fundamental rule about betweeness
(according to hilbert) is therefore broken, but we can easily repair it by subjecting betweeness to these symmetry
constraints. We need to for lines anyway -- as the line `AB` is obviously equal to `BA`.

This leaves us with a structure something like:

```lean
structure Tangling where
    points : FinSet Point
    constraints: FinSet Betweeness
    species: Closed | Half | Open | Cyclic
```

This structure is, in some sense, a geometric primitive; it is also a sheaf for a sufficiently convenient topology (in
this case, the subset topology of the FinSet of points, more on this later). Gluing is natural, restriction is natural
within a specific tangling. We can also introduce gluing _across_ tanglings to turn these primitives into proper
geometric diagrams.

I had read a bit about sheaves before, but always bounced off the fact that I needed so much background algebra and
topology to even approach the cliff face it wanted me to climb. Here it seems I had found a version-in-miniature, a
little bouldering problem, and all that remained was to sit down and actually prove it.

# Working without a net

Sheaves need a couple things. First, we have to pick an underlying space, then we have to identify the topology of that
space, then we have to show that:

1. If there is some $$U$$ an open set of the space, then we have a set $$F(U)$$ which is our 'section' of the space. For us,
   this is any collection of named points from the space of all possible named points. In our case that space is $$\N$$,
   so we're dealing with finite subsets of $$\N$$
2. Given $$V \subseteq U$$, there is some function $$r : F(U) \to F(V)$$ that 'restricts' the values from $$U$$ to $$V$$. In our
   case, this is simple 'forgetting' as we noted above. $$r$$ must satisfy some simple properties (identity and
   composition), which 'forgetting' does (we can break any 'forgetting' operation into a sequence of individual point
   removals, which compose freely, and simply not forgetting anything is the identity).

Adhering to these gets us to a presheaf, the next gets us a sheaf:

3. For every open cover, if two sections restrict to the same result for every open set in the cover, then the two
   sections are identical. This is 'locality' of a sheaf. The data doesn't 'get loose' and affect things far away from
   it.
4. For a pair of sections, given an open cover, if those sections agree everywhere along that cover that they overlap,
   then there is another section that can restrict to either component section[^1].

It's that last item that's tricky. In fact, it's not just one sheaf we have, but a few stacked on top of each other.
The intuition I have found so far around sheaves is that they are sort of best regarded as capturing a 'fact' about some
local section of a structure. So an 'Alignment' sheaf might _only_ carry the collinearity 'fact' of some small set of
named points. The restriction axiom allows us to 'shave down' that local space, and the gluing axiom allows us to 'build
up' the space while maintaining underlying invariants (so called 'coherence'). If you do this correctly, then you create
a structure that can track _local_ properties all the way up to a _global_ level, which is a really big deal as most of
mathematics can be framed as questions about properties that are preserved/rejected/otherwise manipulated in the
transition from local to global[^2].

I'm not going to lay out the proofs here, mostly because I don't yet trust all of them, but this is the path so far.
I follow a relatively common storyline in mathematics. Start with a synthetic theory, devise a structure that models the
theory, then analyze that structure.

What is neat about Tanglings and the tower they sit atop is that they are quite intuitive to think about. Most geometry
problems are really 'how are these points ordered under a variety of conditions' and later 'sort out the equivalence
graph of all the structures in the diagram' -- both of which are fundamentally lattice problems to some extent. Phrasing
things in terms of sheaves also gets us access to powerful constructive tools from Category Theory and Topology, but in
a much more accessible setting than traditional algebraic geometry. In a way, this feels like a 'scale model' of
Algebraic Geometry. We traded size and power for convenience, but the model is 'to scale' in the sense that
(co)homology[^3] still works and can resolve theorems for which it applies. It also exposes a lot of geometry to
decidable algorithms without having to go all the way to quantifier elimination. Most geometric theorems are not massive
clusters of thousands of points; and lattice-walking is pretty well-trod territory.
